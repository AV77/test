01 · The Electrical Nature of Life

A cell is not electrically neutral across its membrane.

−

Resting membrane potential

A voltage difference between the inside and outside of the cell membrane.

⚡

Why it exists

  • Unequal ion distribution
  • Selective membrane permeability
  • Active transport maintains gradients

For a typical neuron, the resting membrane potential is commonly taught as approximately −70 mV (the exact value varies by cell type).

“Resting” does not mean inactive. Ions are distributed unequally, and the membrane is selectively permeable.
01 · Resting state

The ion distribution is unequal.

Outside the cell

Na⁺ relatively high

Cl⁻ relatively high

Ca²⁺ free concentration much higher than inside

Inside the cell

K⁺ relatively high

Large negatively charged proteins and other anions

Free Ca²⁺ kept very low

The resting potential is strongly influenced by potassium permeability in many neurons.

Emphasize relative concentrations, not absolute absence/presence. Chloride distribution differs by cell type and developmental state.
01 · Action potential

A rapid, temporary change in membrane voltage.

An action potential is a brief reversal and recovery of membrane polarity that allows an electrical signal to travel along an excitable membrane.

The sequence below describes a typical neuronal action potential—not every excitable cell.

Define it clearly. Threshold is the level at which regenerative opening of voltage-gated sodium channels produces the all-or-none response.
01 · Phases of a neuronal action potential

Four key events.

1Threshold: depolarization reaches the level that triggers the regenerative response.
2Depolarization: voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open; Na⁺ enters and the membrane potential becomes more positive.
3Repolarization: Na⁺ channels inactivate and K⁺ conductance rises; K⁺ leaves the cell.
4After-hyperpolarization: K⁺ conductance remains elevated briefly; the membrane returns to its resting level.
The sodium-potassium pump does not cause the rapid repolarization of each individual action potential. It helps maintain the gradients over time.

Point to the upstroke, downstroke and undershoot. Explain the ion movements rather than asking the audience to memorize the curve alone.
01 · All-or-none & refractory periods

Threshold determines whether a full action potential occurs.

All-or-none law

If threshold is reached, a full action potential is generated. A stronger stimulus does not make that action potential taller; it can increase firing frequency.

Refractory period

Absolute: another action potential cannot be initiated.

Relative: another can occur, but a stronger-than-usual stimulus is needed.

Absolute refractoriness is associated with inactivated voltage-gated Na channels. Relative refractoriness occurs during recovery, often while the membrane is hyperpolarized.
02 · Sodium & potassium

The voltage duo.

Na⁺

Major extracellular cation

  • Important determinant of extracellular fluid osmolality
  • Inward Na⁺ current drives neuronal depolarization
K⁺

Major intracellular cation

  • Major influence on resting membrane potential
  • Outward K⁺ current contributes to repolarization
Sodium concentration in plasma is closely related to water balance. Potassium is especially important for excitability of nerve and muscle, including cardiac muscle.

The pump transports three sodium ions out for every two potassium ions in, using energy from ATP.

It maintains the concentration gradients that make repeated electrical signalling possible; it is not the fast mechanism of the action-potential upstroke or downstroke.

This is primary active transport. Keep the distinction between maintaining gradients and the rapid channel-mediated currents very clear.
03 · Calcium

Calcium links electrical signals to cellular action.

01

Muscle

A rise in intracellular Ca²⁺ enables the contractile process.

02

Synapse

Ca²⁺ entry at nerve terminals triggers neurotransmitter release.

03

Heart

Ca²⁺ entry contributes to the cardiac action-potential plateau and contraction.

Free cytosolic calcium is kept very low at rest; controlled changes in its concentration act as an intracellular signal.

This is the “contraction switch” idea. Calcium is also important in many other cellular processes, but keep the seminar focused.
04 · Chloride

Chloride is more than a number on a lab report.

Fluid & electrical balance

Cl⁻ is the major extracellular anion and contributes to electroneutrality and fluid distribution.

Neural inhibition

Cl⁻ conductance through GABAA receptors commonly contributes to inhibitory signalling in the mature central nervous system.

The effect of chloride movement on membrane voltage depends on the chloride gradient and the cell type.

Avoid saying chloride always moves into neurons or always hyperpolarizes them. Its direction and effect depend on the chloride equilibrium potential.
05 · Your heart runs on electricity

Cardiac muscle has its own action-potential pattern.

Working ventricular muscle

Phase 0: rapid Na⁺ entry

Phase 1: brief early repolarization

Phase 2: plateau, with Ca²⁺ entry balanced by K⁺ movement

Phase 3: K⁺-mediated repolarization

Phase 4: resting membrane potential

Why the plateau matters

It prolongs the cardiac action potential and refractory period, helping prevent sustained tetanic contraction of the heart muscle.

06 · Potassium imbalance

Potassium disturbances can affect neuromuscular and cardiac function.

Hypokalaemia

Low extracellular K⁺ can cause muscle weakness and alter cardiac repolarization.

ECG associations can include flattened T waves and U waves.

Hyperkalaemia

High extracellular K⁺ can disturb membrane excitability and cardiac conduction.

ECG changes can include tall peaked T waves, PR prolongation and QRS widening as severity increases.

Either disturbance can be clinically important because serious cardiac rhythm abnormalities may occur.

Do not imply every patient follows a textbook ECG sequence. ECG findings vary, and a normal ECG does not reliably exclude a dangerous potassium abnormality.
06 · Sodium imbalance

Sodium disorders are closely linked to water balance.

Hyponatraemia

Low plasma sodium concentration usually reflects excess water relative to exchangeable body sodium and potassium.

A fall in extracellular osmolality can cause water to move into brain cells, producing neurological symptoms.

Hypernatraemia

High plasma sodium concentration usually reflects a deficit of water relative to body solute.

Increased extracellular osmolality draws water out of brain cells and can cause neurological dysfunction.

Avoid describing sodium concentration as simply “the amount of sodium in the body.” It is strongly influenced by the relationship between body solute and water.
06 · Calcium & chloride

Different ions, different clinical patterns.

Calcium

Low calcium: increased neuromuscular excitability; may cause tingling, cramps, tetany and, in significant cases, seizures or cardiac effects.

High calcium: may cause gastrointestinal, renal, neurological and cardiovascular effects.

Chloride

Chloride abnormalities often accompany changes in sodium, water balance and acid–base status.

Low chloride may occur with metabolic alkalosis; high chloride may occur in some forms of metabolic acidosis.

Keep the chloride section proportionate: its clinical interpretation is often contextual, alongside sodium and bicarbonate/acid-base assessment.
06 · The clinical takeaway

One disturbance can affect more than one system.

Nervous system

Confusion, altered consciousness, seizures or altered neuromuscular excitability, depending on the disturbance.

Muscles

Weakness, cramps, altered reflexes or tetany in relevant electrolyte disorders.

Heart

Changes in conduction and repolarization; potentially serious arrhythmias, particularly with potassium abnormalities.

Clinical interpretation depends on the ion involved, the magnitude and speed of change, the underlying cause and the patient's overall condition.

This is the central clinical message. Electrolytes are not isolated lab values; they are part of the physiology of excitable tissues and fluid balance.
